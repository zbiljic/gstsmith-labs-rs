Straightened displays become upright rectangles of their own aspect ratio, turned only in quarter turns, so each shows a plain crop of the video. Small rotations and perspective in the photo, or in the mounting, are ignored.

4. Play

Files on this server (file:///…), HTTP(S) videos, HLS and DASH playlists, RTSP, SRT and RTMP cameras and encoders, WHEP endpoints (URLs ending in /whep), and YouTube and other pages yt-dlp knows, which take a few seconds to look up. WHEP endpoints play without sound.

Applies while playing, without reconnecting; Safari ignores it.
Plays on one display; the others get it muted, so lip sync holds every display's video back alike. Turning it on or off restarts playback.

Changing the source, the standby or the restart setting, or moving the sound to another display, applies at once while playing; the displays keep their streams.

5. Measure and align

Each display reports which frame it presents, from the frame's RTP timestamp in Chrome and Edge or else from a frame number drawn in its bottom-left corner only while such a display needs it, and when its browser expects every frame on screen, on this server's clock. Latency runs from a frame's capture: Pipeline until it is warped and ready to encode, Arrives until its last packet reaches the browser, On screen until it is expected on screen; Jitter buffer is the browser's own buffering, part of the last step. Offsets compare displays for the same frame; the offset change is how far that moved across the window, and one refresh, about 16.7 ms at 60 Hz, means a display slipped a frame. Panel latency is not included.

Align displays measures too, and gives every display that presents frames earlier than the latest one that much more jitter buffer, a step every few seconds, until all agree within a refresh or so; Added delay is each display's share. It needs browsers that honor a jitter buffer target, which Safari does not, and it is only as exact as each display's clock synchronization (Clock ±).
